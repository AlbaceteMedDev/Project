The uncomfortable finding

The strongest single indicator at three of four positions is last year's points per game. Not an efficiency metric, not a usage rate — the fantasy score itself. Everything else on this page earns its place by telling you which good season was real: target share and yards per route separate a receiver who earned his points from one who caught six touchdowns on forty targets.

Tested and rejected

A coaching change, a move to a better quarterback, a team change and a player's draft round were all tested as features. Adding a coaching change made the model worse at every position (QB 0.710 → 0.696). A quarterback upgrade was worth nothing once the box score was known. Two survived: a receiver who missed most of last season (0.922 → 0.929, now in the model) and a quarterback changing teams (0.710 → 0.737, left out — next year's roster is not in this data).

Also rejected: how he finished. Season totals hide whether a player was steady or surging, so the last six games of each prior season were rebuilt from the weekly files and tested. They add nothing at any position — WR 0.929 → 0.928, TE 0.881 → 0.862 with all five late-window features. "He finished strong" is a story, not a signal.

How to read a row

Three numbers, and they answer different questions.

The big number

Probability of a top-8 finish, from a model scored leave-one-season-out — every season predicted by a version that never trained on it. It is calibrated: across eleven seasons, players scored near 0.30 finished top-8 about 30% of the time. This is the only figure here validated out of sample, so it sets the tier.

Gates

How much of the elite in-season profile his most recent played season looked like. Descriptive, not held out — a miss is a question to answer, not a disqualification. A player who missed last season is scored on the one before, and the row says so.

Leaper marks

How closely he matches the players who jumped into the top 8 from outside the prior top 12. Roughly 40% of all elite finishes came from there, so this is the late-round signal. It is shown only for players in that lane — the bars sit at the leapers' own medians, so anyone who was already top-12 clears all of them and the count would mean nothing.

What is missing

The specific gates his prior year failed. Read it as the thesis you are buying: something has to change for that line to clear.

The route estimate is the one metric to discount. Public data has no route counts, so routes come from snap share × team dropbacks. Receivers who leave the field on run downs — Nacua and Smith-Njigba most obviously — run routes on a far higher share of dropbacks than their snap share implies, so a "routes per game" miss on that kind of player is usually the proxy failing, not the player.

Before you use it

  • Nobody here is a lock. The top score on the whole board is 0.87 — even that one misses roughly one year in ten, and below the first handful of names the odds fall away fast. The base rate at wide receiver is 4.6%, so the very top of this board is a large edge on a small chance.
  • The pool quietly drops the injured. A player has to log a scoreable season to be counted, so 173 receiver-seasons, 138 running backs and 82 tight ends vanished from the denominator after one qualifying year. Counting those as failures, the real receiver base rate is 4.0%, not 4.6% — and every probability here is a shade optimistic for the same reason.
  • It cannot see a job that did not exist yet. Rookies have no NFL history to score and are structurally invisible. Two seasons of lookback closed most of the injury gap — 86–94% of past top-8 seasons are now reachable — but a first-year player never will be.
  • It cannot see availability. Suspensions, holdouts and training-camp injuries are not in the data. Anyone facing league discipline or rehabbing an injury is scored as though he plays a full season, which is exactly when this board is most wrong.
  • The depth chart is current; everything else is last year's. Each row carries the player's rank on his team's August 2026 chart, which is the only forward-looking fact here. It is also the sharpest one — a receiver listed second finishes top-8 0.4% of the time against 7.1% for the one listed first. Below the top slot the ordering is loose, especially at receiver where teams play three: the 2026 Jaguars list Travis Hunter fourth. Read a low rank as a question, not a verdict.
  • Team context is last year's. Anyone who changed teams this offseason still carries his old offense's scoring rank and quarterback quality.
  • Story lines are not in it, and mostly should not be. A coaching change, a move to a better quarterback and a change of team were each tested as model features. A coaching change made the model worse at every position. The one narrative that earned a place is a receiver who missed most of last season, which the box-score features over-punish on their own.
  • Efficiency does not rescue a small role. 176 receiver-seasons had a prior year under a 15% target share and 50% snaps. None finished top-8 the next year. None finished top-12.
