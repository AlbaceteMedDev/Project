# Etiology Playbooks — Assessment and Plan by Wound Type

The generic template covers what every wound needs. This file covers what
*this* wound needs — because the single most important intervention is different
for each type, and getting it wrong means a perfect dressing on a wound that
was never going to heal.

| Wound type | The one thing that decides the outcome |
| --- | --- |
| Sacral / coccygeal pressure injury | **Shear and moisture control** — HOB angle, not the dressing |
| Trochanteric pressure injury | **Never 90° side-lying** — 30° tilt, off that side entirely |
| Ischial pressure injury | **Sitting time and the seating system** |
| Heel pressure injury | **Perfusion status before you debride anything** |
| Venous leg ulcer | **Compression, actually worn** |
| Diabetic foot ulcer | **Offloading, preferably irremovable** |
| Arterial / ischemic ulcer | **Revascularization — and do no harm until then** |

---

## 0. Before the plan: is this wound healable?

Decide this first and state it in the note, because it changes everything
downstream. A wound treated on the wrong track gets the wrong care with the best
intentions.

| Classification | Criteria | What the plan looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Healable** | Adequate perfusion, and the cause can be corrected | Full active treatment: moist wound healing, debridement to a viable base, correction of the cause, advanced therapies where indicated |
| **Maintenance** | Healable in principle, but patient, caregiver, resource, or system factors prevent the required care — or the patient declines it | Keep it stable and prevent deterioration. Reduce the regimen to what will actually happen. Name the barrier and revisit it at every visit — this is a status, not a verdict |
| **Non-healable / palliative** | Perfusion cannot be corrected, or the patient is at end of life or has a terminal illness with a wound that will outlive the healing capacity | **Do not pursue moist wound healing.** Keep the wound **dry**, preserve dry stable eschar, avoid sharp debridement to a bleeding base. Manage odor, pain, exudate, and bleeding. Protect dignity |

**Write the classification into the note explicitly.** In a non-healable wound,
keeping an eschar dry and intact is correct care — but only if the record says
that was the decision and why. Otherwise the next clinician reads it as neglect.

> **Wound classified as [healable / maintenance / non-healable]** based on
> [perfusion: ABI/TBI [X]], [correctability of the cause], and [goals of care
> discussion with [name] on [date]]. Treatment is therefore directed at
> [healing / stability and prevention of deterioration / comfort, odor and
> exudate control, and preservation of dry stable eschar]. This was discussed
> with [patient / family], who [verbalized understanding and agreed].

## 1. Sacral and coccygeal pressure injury

### What actually causes it

Not pressure alone — **shear**. When the head of the bed is elevated, the patient
slides down while the skin over the sacrum stays put. The tissue layers move
against each other and the perforating vessels kink. Add moisture from
incontinence, which softens the stratum corneum and lowers the tissue's
tolerance, and you have the classic sacral ulcer: undermined at the superior
margin, in a patient whose bed is kept at 45°.

### Assessment specifics

- **Stage** (NPIAP): 1, 2, 3, 4, unstageable, or **deep tissue pressure injury**
  (persistent non-blanchable deep red, maroon, or purple discoloration — may
  deteriorate rapidly even with correct care; say so in advance).
- **Distinguish from incontinence-associated dermatitis.** IAD is diffuse with
  indistinct borders over any moisture-exposed skin, blanchable, often with
  satellite lesions from candida. A pressure injury sits over the bony
  prominence with a distinct edge. They coexist constantly and are treated
  differently — chart which you are looking at.
- **Head-of-bed angle observed, not asked.** Record what you found on arrival.
- **Does the patient slide?** Look for the linen bunched at the foot, and for
  undermining at the superior margin — the shear signature.
- Continence status, **stool consistency** (liquid stool is a different problem
  from formed), current containment products and change frequency.
- Sitting tolerance and current chair time.
- Braden, with attention to the **moisture and friction/shear subscores**.

### Plan

1. **HOB ≤ 30° at all times except during meals; lowered within 30 minutes after
   eating.** Use the knee gatch or foot-of-bed elevation to stop sliding.
2. **Q2H repositioning at 30° lateral tilt**, alternating sides, never supine on
   the ulcer.
3. **Lift sheet with two staff for every boost — never drag.** Dragging is the
   mechanism; a lift sheet is the fix.
4. **Prophylactic or therapeutic silicone foam sacral dressing** as a
   shear-redistribution layer, changed per manufacturer guidance and lifted for
   skin inspection at least daily.
5. **Bowel and bladder program**: scheduled toileting, check-and-change interval
   stated in hours, barrier film or paste at every change. For persistent liquid
   stool, evaluate for a **fecal management system** — repeated soiling of a
   Stage 3–4 sacral wound is an indication, not a convenience.
6. **Group 2 support surface** with the justification documented; pressure
   redistribution cushion for chair time; **no donut or ring devices**.
7. Sitting limited to a stated interval with weight shifts every 15 minutes.
8. Nutrition per §6 of the clinical reference.

### Red flags

Undermining at the superior margin that grows week over week means the shear has
not been fixed — recheck the HOB and the transfer technique before changing the
dressing. New purple discoloration at the margin is deep tissue injury extending.
Foul odor with fever in a Stage 4 sacral wound, near the ischium or rectum, needs
imaging and evaluation for deep abscess or osteomyelitis.

## 2. Trochanteric (hip) pressure injury

**Cause:** 90° side-lying, which puts full body weight on the greater trochanter
where there is almost no tissue between skin and bone.

**Assessment:** as above, plus which side the patient prefers, whether contracture
forces the position, and how the ulcer relates to the mattress and side rails.

**Plan:**
1. **30° lateral tilt with pillow bridging behind the back. 90° side-lying is
   prohibited** — write it that way, because "reposition side to side" will be
   read as permission.
2. **No positioning on the affected side at all** until healed — alternate the
   opposite tilt and supine.
3. Pillow between the knees and ankles to prevent bony contact.
4. Address contractures with PT/OT — if the hip will not abduct, the tilt cannot
   be achieved and the order will silently fail.
5. Support surface, nutrition, and turn-log audit as above.

**Red flag:** deep undermining tracking toward the hip joint, or a wound that
probes to bone at the trochanter, needs imaging — trochanteric osteomyelitis and
septic arthritis of the hip both present this way.

## 3. Ischial pressure injury

**Cause:** sitting. These occur in people who spend their day in a wheelchair,
and they recur at high rates because the exposure returns every day.

**Assessment:** hours seated per day, the cushion (type, age, condition — a
cushion that has bottomed out is doing nothing), the ability to perform
independent weight shifts, transfer technique, and posture (a posterior pelvic
tilt loads the ischium directly). These wounds are typically deep with extensive
undermining and a small surface opening.

**Plan:**
1. **Sitting time limited to a stated number of minutes per session** with a
   defined out-of-chair interval.
2. **Weight shift every 15 minutes** — forward lean, side lean, or push-up
   depending on ability. Set a timer or a phone cue; "as able" will not happen.
3. **Seating and positioning evaluation** with pressure mapping where available;
   tilt-in-space or recline wheelchair; replace a bottomed-out cushion.
4. Probe and measure undermining every visit — the surface opening
   underestimates these wounds badly.
5. After healing, the seating plan continues indefinitely. Say so, and document
   the recurrence-prevention plan as part of the discharge.

## 4. Heel pressure injury

**The rule that matters: assess perfusion before you touch it.** The heel has
thin tissue over bone and is frequently ischemic in exactly the population that
develops these wounds.

**Assessment:** pulses, capillary refill, temperature, **ABI or toe pressure**,
and whether the eschar is **dry, hard, and stable** versus **boggy, fluctuant,
draining, or surrounded by erythema**.

**Plan:**
1. **Float the heels completely off the surface** — pillow under the full length
   of the calf with the knee slightly flexed so the heel hangs free, or a heel
   offloading boot. Check that a hand passes freely under the heel.
2. **Dry, stable eschar on an ischemic heel is left intact.** It is acting as a
   biological cover. Do not debride it, do not hydrate it, keep it dry — this is
   the one setting where painting with povidone-iodine to maintain dryness is
   appropriate. Document the perfusion finding and the decision, or the next
   reader will treat it as an oversight.
3. **Reassess daily for the signs that change the plan**: fluctuance, drainage,
   surrounding erythema, odor, or new pain. Any of these means the eschar is no
   longer protective and needs urgent evaluation.
4. If perfusion is adequate and the eschar is unstable, debride.
5. Vascular referral if the ABI is under 0.9, or for any non-healing heel wound.
6. **No donut devices**, and avoid pressure from footwear, boots, or bed linen
   pulled tight over the toes.

## 5. Venous leg ulcer

### Recognition

Gaiter area, usually near the medial malleolus. Shallow with an irregular border,
a granulating base, and **heavy exudate**. Surrounding skin shows the disease:
hemosiderin staining, lipodermatosclerosis (the inverted-champagne-bottle calf),
atrophie blanche, ankle flare, varicosities, and edema. Pain is usually
moderate and **improves with elevation** — the opposite of arterial pain.

### Assessment specifics

- **ABI is mandatory before any compression.** Document the value and the date.
- Edema character (pitting versus fibrotic), Stemmer sign for a lymphatic
  component.
- Duration, recurrence history, and prior compression tolerance.
- **Ask specifically whether the wrap comes off between visits, and why.**
  Non-adherence here is usually pain, heat, sleep, or bathing — each has a
  different fix.
- Periwound dermatitis: these patients have high rates of contact sensitization
  to topical antibiotics, lanolin, fragrance, and adhesives.

### Compression thresholds

| ABI | Compression |
| --- | --- |
| **≥ 0.8** | Full strength, 30–40 mmHg at the ankle, graduated |
| **0.5 – 0.79** | Modified, 23–30 mmHg, with close monitoring and vascular consultation |
| **< 0.5** | **No compression.** Urgent vascular referral |
| **> 1.3** | Non-compressible vessels — the ABI is unreliable. Obtain toe pressure or TBI before deciding |

### Plan

1. **Compression is the treatment**, and multilayer outperforms single layer.
   Everything else is adjunctive.
2. **Elevation above heart level**, prescribed in specific sessions per day, not
   "as tolerated."
3. **Calf-pump exercise**: ankle pumps hourly while awake, leg raises several
   times daily. The calf muscle pump is the physiological engine of venous
   return, and in an immobile patient it has stopped.
4. Exudate management sized to the drainage, with a periwound barrier — maceration
   here is a dressing failure, not an infection.
5. **Emollients to the surrounding skin** at every change; the skin is
   chronically dry and fissures become the next ulcer.
6. **Venous duplex ultrasound and referral for superficial venous ablation** —
   early endovenous intervention alongside compression speeds healing and reduces
   recurrence. This is a referral to make early, not after failure.
7. Consider **pentoxifylline** as an adjunct to compression.
8. Weight management and treatment of heart failure where they drive the edema.
9. **After healing, compression stockings continue indefinitely.** Recurrence
   without them is the expected outcome, not a complication. Build the fitting
   into the discharge plan.

### When it does not heal

Before escalating to advanced therapy, verify in this order: **is the compression
actually being worn?** Then recheck the **ABI**. Then look for **contact
dermatitis** from the current products. Then — for any venous ulcer failing to
progress despite adequate compression — **biopsy the edge** to exclude
malignancy and vasculitis.

## 6. Diabetic foot ulcer

### Recognition

Plantar surface over the metatarsal heads, the hallux, or the heel, in a foot
with **loss of protective sensation**. Typically a punched-out wound with a
**callus rim**, minimal pain, and a deceptively small surface opening over a
larger cavity.

### Assessment specifics

- **Neuropathy:** 10 g Semmes-Weinstein monofilament at defined sites, 128 Hz
  tuning fork, or vibration perception threshold. Document loss of protective
  sensation explicitly — it is the reason the patient kept walking on it.
- **Vascular:** pulses, ABI, **and toe-brachial index or toe pressure**.
  ⚠️ **An ABI above 1.3 in a diabetic patient means medial arterial
  calcification, not good perfusion.** Do not accept it. TBI below 0.7 is
  abnormal; toe pressure under 30 mmHg predicts non-healing.
- **Probe to bone** with a sterile blunt probe. A positive probe-to-bone in an
  infected DFU substantially raises the likelihood of osteomyelitis.
- **Classification:** Wagner grade or University of Texas (which grades depth
  against infection and ischemia — more useful for prognosis).
- **Callus, deformity, and prior amputation.** Rocker-bottom (Charcot),
  hammertoes, prominent metatarsal heads, hallux valgus, equinus.
- **Inspect the footwear at the visit** — inside the shoe, by hand.
- Interdigital spaces and nails; check both feet, every visit.

### Plan

1. **Offloading is the treatment.** In order of effectiveness:
   **total contact cast** (the reference standard for a non-infected,
   non-ischemic plantar ulcer) → **irremovable cast walker** → removable cast
   walker → healing sandal or postoperative shoe → felted foam. The gap between
   removable and irremovable devices is adherence, and it is large. If a
   removable device is chosen, document why.
2. **Debride the callus at every visit.** Callus concentrates plantar pressure
   and delays closure; it is not protective.
3. **Glycemic control** with an individualized target, communicated to the
   managing clinician; address variability, not only the A1c.
4. **Infection assessment at every visit**, with tissue culture rather than a
   surface swab. Probe-to-bone positive or a non-healing wound over bone →
   MRI, and bone biopsy where it will change therapy.
5. **Vascular referral** for ABI < 0.9, TBI < 0.7, non-compressible vessels, or
   any wound failing to progress by 4 weeks.
6. **Podiatry co-management** and, at healing, **custom insoles and therapeutic
   footwear**.
7. **Daily foot inspection** by the patient or caregiver, with a mirror or a
   phone camera for the plantar surface. Never barefoot, including indoors.
   Check inside shoes by hand before each wear.

### After healing

Recurrence is high — a substantial share of healed DFUs recur within a year. The
plan continues: therapeutic footwear, ongoing podiatry, daily inspection, and
prompt review of any new callus or blister. **Document the wound as "in
remission" rather than "resolved"**, and say what the surveillance interval is.

## 7. Arterial / ischemic ulcer

### Recognition

Distal — toes, lateral malleolus, or pressure points. **Punched-out** with sharp
edges, a pale, dry, or necrotic base, and **minimal exudate**. **Painful**, with
rest pain that is **worse on elevation and relieved by dependency** — the
opposite of venous. Cool foot, absent pulses, dependent rubor, pallor on
elevation, hair loss, thin shiny skin, thickened nails.

### The rules

1. **Do not compress.**
2. **Do not debride dry stable eschar.** Without perfusion, debridement creates a
   larger wound that cannot heal and opens a portal for infection.
3. **Revascularization comes first.** ABI < 0.5, rest pain, or tissue loss is
   critical limb-threatening ischemia — this is an **urgent vascular referral**,
   not a wound-clinic problem.

### Plan

Keep the wound dry and protected. Protect from pressure and trauma. Aggressive
cardiovascular risk management: smoking cessation, statin, antiplatelet,
blood-pressure and glycemic control, with the managing clinician. Pain control
that is actually adequate — ischemic rest pain is severe. Treat infection
promptly and systemically, because local defenses are poor. Then reassess the
wound plan **after** revascularization, at which point a previously non-healable
wound may become healable and the whole plan changes.

**Warning signs requiring same-day evaluation**, given to the patient in writing:
increasing rest pain, new numbness, the foot turning blue, black, or white,
spreading redness, or fever.

## 8. Mixed venous-arterial ulcer

Common and easy to get wrong in both directions. The venous features are present,
but so is arterial disease.

- **ABI 0.5–0.79:** modified compression at 23–30 mmHg, with vascular
  consultation and close monitoring. Document the ABI, the reduced pressure, and
  the monitoring plan in the same paragraph.
- **ABI < 0.5:** treat as arterial. No compression.
- Reassess the ABI if the wound deteriorates or the patient develops rest pain.
- Educate the patient on what to report — pain that increases after wrapping,
  numbness, or color change — and make clear that with mixed disease, this is
  reported and not self-managed.

## 9. The deeper assessment and plan

The generic A&P in the template covers the structure. For a complex patient,
these are the elements that make it a clinical document rather than a summary.

### Assessment — the seven things worth stating

1. **The patient in one sentence** — age, the comorbidities that bear on healing,
   and the functional status that determines what is achievable.
2. **The wound in one sentence** — location, etiology, classification, duration,
   current size, and post-debridement bed.
3. **The trajectory, with the benchmark named**, and whether it is met.
4. **Whether the diagnosis still fits.** A wound that fails a correct plan is
   telling you something. State that you considered it: perfusion reassessed,
   osteomyelitis excluded, biopsy considered.
5. **The healability classification** and the goals of care behind it.
6. **The impediments, ranked** — not an undifferentiated list. Which one is
   actually holding this wound back? For a sacral ulcer with perfect dressings
   and an HOB at 45°, it is the shear, and the note should say so.
7. **What has changed since the last visit and why.**

### Plan — the eight things worth stating

1. **The single highest-yield intervention**, named as such. Every plan has one.
2. Local wound care with the reasoning for the dressing choice.
3. Cause correction — offloading, compression, revascularization, glycemic
   control — with the specific parameters, not the category.
4. Systemic optimization: nutrition, infection, comorbidities, medication review.
5. **Measurable goals with a timeframe.** "Expect ≥ 30% area reduction by
   [date]" is a goal. "Continue current care" is not.
6. **Escalation triggers, stated in advance** — the specific findings that change
   the plan, so that nursing and the next provider act without waiting.
7. Care coordination: who was contacted, about what, and what they are doing.
8. **Next visit and what will be reassessed at it.**

### Ready-to-paste structure

> **ASSESSMENT:** [Age] [sex] with [comorbidities bearing on healing] and
> [functional status], with a [duration] [classification] [location] wound,
> currently [size] sq cm ([X]% cumulative reduction; [Y]% over four weeks against
> a ≥[N]% benchmark). Wound bed [X]% granulation following today's debridement.
> **Trajectory [on track / stalled / deteriorating].** Wound is classified
> **[healable / maintenance / non-healable]** on the basis of [perfusion,
> correctability, goals of care].
> The diagnosis remains [etiology]; [perfusion was reassessed / osteomyelitis was
> excluded / biopsy was considered and is [indicated / not indicated]] because
> [reason].
> **The rate-limiting impediment is [X]** — [why], and it is addressed by [Y].
> Additional impediments: [ranked list].
>
> **PLAN:**
> 1. **Highest-yield intervention:** [X], because [why].
> 2. Local wound care: [dressing] because [wound state requires property].
> 3. Cause correction: [specific parameters].
> 4. Systemic: [nutrition, infection, comorbidity, medications].
> 5. **Goal: [measurable target] by [date].**
> 6. **Escalate immediately for:** [specific findings] → [action], contact
>    [name, number].
> 7. Coordination: [who was contacted, about what, and their response].
> 8. **Next visit [date]**, at which I will reassess [specific items].
